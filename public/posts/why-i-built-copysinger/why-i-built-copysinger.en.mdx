## Turning Voice Cloning into a Product Without Per-User Training

I have been interested in TTS and voice cloning for a long time. The process of reproducing someone's timbre from a few short recordings fascinated me, and I wanted to turn the technology into a web service of my own.

The cost barrier appeared as soon as I explored implementation options. The training-based SVC (Singing Voice Conversion) approaches I considered required collecting and preprocessing voice samples for each user, fine-tuning a separate model on a GPU, and managing dedicated `.pth` weights.

Running a training pipeline for every user was not sustainable for a personal project, both in GPU cost and waiting time. I wanted to build a voice cloning product, but the project was becoming an exercise in operating model-training infrastructure.

## SoulX-Singer: Moving to a Zero-Shot Model

The breakthrough was [`SoulX-Singer`](https://github.com/Soul-AILab/SoulX-Singer). SoulX-Singer-SVC is an open-source zero-shot model that can convert a song into the timbre of a short reference recording without additional training for each user.

The key change was a simpler workflow. Once per-user training disappeared, a `user voice reference + target vocal track` could produce a result through a single inference pipeline.

![Comparison between per-user data preparation and GPU training in training-based SVC and SoulX-Singer-SVC zero-shot inference from a short reference](/public/posts/why-i-built-copysinger/training-vs-zero-shot.en.webp)

Zero-shot inference still needs GPU compute and remains sensitive to noise and reference quality. What disappeared was the structural bottleneck of training a model every time a new voice was registered. That let me move my attention from training-infrastructure cost to an experience people could actually use.

## From a Demo to a Product: The Missing Song Choice

I first built a small demo that validated only the minimum pipeline.

1. Separate the vocals and accompaniment from the original song
2. Convert the isolated vocals into the user's timbre with SoulX-Singer-SVC
3. Remix the converted vocals with the accompaniment

The output worked, but using the demo revealed its limits.

* Users did not know which songs would fit their voices.
* If the original key did not fit, it was unclear how many semitones to move it.
* When a result sounded unnatural, it was difficult to tell whether the reference quality or the range mismatch was responsible.

A converter that accepted two files risked becoming a demo people tried once for fun rather than a product they would keep using.

## Voice Analysis and Song-Catalog Matching

I changed `Copysinger` to fill that decision gap. Instead of converting audio immediately, the service first analyzes the user's range, recommends suitable songs and keys, and then connects that choice to AI mixing.

![Copysinger home screen](/public/projects/copysinger/copysinger-home.png)

When a user records a short phrase in the browser, the analysis pipeline—including librosa's pYIN algorithm—extracts the following metrics.

* **Observed range:** The lowest and highest notes detected in the recording
* **Core range:** The pitch region observed most frequently among valid frames
* **Center pitch and pitch trajectory:** The median valid pitch and how pitch changes over time

![Copysinger product flow from a short recording and song catalog through shared analysis and recommendations to user-selected AI mixing](/public/posts/why-i-built-copysinger/product-flow.en.webp)

One important UI decision was to avoid presenting the result as a judgment of singing ability. A short recording cannot establish someone's absolute skill or voice type. Copysinger therefore does not assign a score to singing ability or classify a voice type. Its recommendation score is only a reference value describing how the range observed in this recording relates to a song's range, accompanied by the semitone shift that moves the song into a more comfortable region.

## Separating Analysis Audio from the Synthesis Reference

During development, I found that the same recording could not be used unchanged for both analysis and synthesis. The two tasks needed different properties from the input.

* **Voice analysis:** The full natural distribution, from low to high notes, must be preserved to measure the recorded range.
* **SoulX-Singer-SVC synthesis:** A longer reference is not automatically better. Breathing noise, silence, unstable pitch, and clipped segments can distort the output.

I split the preprocessing of a single input into two paths.

1. **Analysis pipeline:** Preserve the complete pitch distribution and create the vocal profile from the full recording
2. **Synthesis-reference pipeline:** Select midrange segments that satisfy voiced-density, loudness, and clipping criteria, then join them in their original order. The reference is capped at 30 seconds; if less good material is available, the pipeline uses the shorter result without repetition or padding from other ranges

![Preprocessing split from one recording into a full-distribution analysis path and a SoulX-Singer-SVC synthesis path using up to 30 seconds of stable midrange audio](/public/posts/why-i-built-copysinger/preprocessing-split.en.webp)

After this split, stable midrange-only references produced cleaner synthesis than references that distributed time evenly across low, middle, and high ranges.

## A Durable Job Lifecycle with Modal and PostgreSQL

Pitch analysis takes tens of seconds, while SVC inference and mixing take roughly three to four minutes per song. Handling that pipeline inside a single HTTP request would make it vulnerable to browser exits, network interruptions, and server restarts.

I separated the work into asynchronous durable queues.

![Durable audio job flow from web request through a PostgreSQL queue and lease worker to Modal CPU analysis, L4 GPU mixing, and reconnectable result storage](/public/posts/why-i-built-copysinger/durable-job-flow.en.webp)

* **State and durable queues:** The web server accepts a request, inserts it into a PostgreSQL queue, and immediately returns a job ID. A worker claims the job, and if that worker stops unexpectedly, another worker can recover it after the lease expires.
* **Compute separation with Modal:**
  * CPU-intensive pitch analysis and preprocessing run in Modal's CPU environment.
  * SoulX-Singer-SVC inference runs on an on-demand L4 GPU.
  * Containers scale to zero when unused, reducing idle GPU cost and keeping the resources for each task separate.
* **Storage separation with Leemage:** Large source audio and converted results are stored in [Leemage](https://leemage.leey00nsu.com/), a media platform I built. PostgreSQL keeps metadata and file URL references instead of the audio binaries.

I also calculated the operating cost from observed execution time. As of August 2026, the [Modal Starter plan](https://modal.com/pricing) includes $30 of free compute credit each month. An L4 costs $0.000222 per second, and a Copysinger mix takes about three to four minutes, putting GPU execution at roughly $0.040–$0.053 per song. Based only on active GPU time, the free credit could theoretically cover about 560–750 mixes per month.

That number is an upper bound. CPU and memory usage, cold starts, and the time a container remains warm after a job are billed separately, so actual capacity is lower. Voice analysis runs without a GPU, and six remote benchmark runs put its average compute cost below $0.001 per analysis. Compared with GPU mixing, its contribution to the overall budget is small.

With this structure, a job can continue safely after the user closes the browser, and the completed result remains available when they return.

## Closing Thoughts

`Copysinger` turned an idea I had postponed because of per-user GPU training costs into a working service by using an open-source zero-shot model.

Model quality mattered, but it was only one part of the product. Input preprocessing, the analysis and recommendation experience, and a backend lifecycle that moves heavy AI work outside the web request were all necessary to make the model usable as a service.
